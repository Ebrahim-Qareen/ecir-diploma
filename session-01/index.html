    How every session works

    Every topic is taught the same way, so the method becomes a habit. It starts with six questions — the ones an analyst asks about any alert.

    One habit

    On any alert, ask six simple questions. Click each one to see what it means on shift.

    1 · What happened?

    Say it in one line from the evidence, not from the alert name: "15 failed logins, then one success, from one IP."

    2 · Where?

    The host and its role. A laptop or the critical file server? The role changes the severity.

    3 · Who?

    The account touched and the account used. Is it a real user, a service account, an admin?

    4 · When?

    First event, last event, and what came after. A login at 03:00 followed by file access is a story, not two alerts.

    5 · How?

    Name the technique and map it to MITRE ATT&CK, e.g. T1110 Brute Force. The ID lets anyone read your report.

    6 · So what?

    Impact and the next action: close, escalate, contain? Every answer ends with a decision.

    The SOC and its four pillars

    A SOC is not a room full of screens. It is people, processes, tools and intelligence working together, around the clock.

    What INE says

    A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a centralized unit/team responsible for monitoring, detecting, analyzing and responding to cybersecurity threats across applications, devices, systems, networks and locations. It is the organization's first line of defense — the nerve center of all cybersecurity efforts — and must operate 24/7.

    SOCterm
    Definition
    One central team that watches the whole organisation for attacks and responds to them.
    Why it exists
    Attacks happen at any hour, on any system. Without one central team, alerts are seen by nobody — or by ten people who each think someone else will act.
    Where used
    Banks, telecoms, hospitals, government — anywhere downtime or data loss is expensive.
    Example
    A bank SOC watches 5,000 laptops, 300 servers and the cloud from one dashboard, 24 hours a day.
    SOC example
    At 02:10 the SIEM raises a Critical alert. The night-shift Tier 1 analyst — not the server owner, who is asleep — starts triage within minutes.
    Think of it like

    A fire brigade. Firefighters (people) follow a drill for each kind of fire (processes), use trucks and alarms (tools), and know which building stores chemicals (intelligence). Take away any one and the fire wins.

    Keep this

    People · Processes · Tools · Intelligence. A SOC with great tools but no process is just expensive noise.

    What makes a SOC work — characteristics and core functions

    Four characteristics tell you whether a SOC is functional. Five core functions tell you how it turns raw logs into action.

    Four key characteristics

    1 · Centralized command center

    One team, 24/7, on a unified tool set (SIEM, EDR, IDS/IPS, firewalls). Central visibility lets it correlate events, and IT and executives rely on it for incident coordination.

    2 · Continuous monitoring

    Real-time watching of traffic, system logs, endpoints and cloud: log collection, SIEM alerting and prioritisation, threat-intel feeds for known IOCs, UEBA for behaviour, NDR and EDR.

    3 · Incident response & containment

    Structured handling (NIST 800-61 or SANS), containment (quarantine, revoke access, block IPs/domains), eradication, forensic investigation, and post-incident reports.

    4 · Coordination & collaboration

    The SOC never works alone: IT teams, executives, legal and compliance (GDPR, HIPAA, PCI-DSS, ISO 27001), HR for awareness, and lessons learned shared back.

    Why it matters

    These four characteristics are the baseline for judging whether a SOC is functional — and where it needs to improve.

    How mature is a SOC?

    Maturity measures how capable and effective a SOC is — from "no SOC, firefighting" to "intel-driven and automated".

    What is SOC maturity?

    A maturity model defines a SOC's capability, effectiveness and operational maturity in detecting, responding to and mitigating incidents. A mature SOC is proactive, automated and aligned with industry frameworks.

    Why it matters

    Scales with new threats · cuts response time · aligns with best practice and compliance · measures progress and finds gaps.

    Measuring a SOC — minutes and noise

    A maturity level is a label. Metrics are the proof. Two questions matter most: how fast does the SOC act, and how much of its work is noise?

    Why measure?

    SOC-CMM has a whole Metrics domain, and the threat-intel table on page 16 promises a lower time to detect and time to respond. You can only improve what you measure.

    MetricFormulaToo high means…Typical fix
    MTTDalert time − attack startattackers work unseenbetter detection rules, real-time log ingestion
    MTTAanalyst start − alert timealerts wait in the queuereal-time notification, balance the queue between analysts
    MTTRcontainment − alert timedamage keeps growingfast escalation, written playbooks, SOAR
    FPR — false-positive ratefalse positives ÷ all alerts × 100alert fatigue: real alerts get losttune rules, exclude trusted activity, automate common triage
    Two notes

    "Mean" = the average over many incidents. With one incident, it is just that incident's time. Definitions vary: each SOC writes its own start and end points — and its targets — in the SLA, the agreement between the SOC and the business (or between an MSSP and its customer). Use the definitions on this page in class.

    Calculate it

    Use the four times in the figure (one incident, so mean = this incident's time). Then this week's noise: 120 alerts, 90 of them false positives. MTTD? MTTA? MTTR? FPR?

    MTTD = how fast you see it. MTTA = how fast you start. MTTR = how fast you stop it. FPR = how much of the queue is noise.

    Specialists around the tiers — hunters and forensics

    Around the three tiers sit specialists: threat hunters look forward, forensics looks back. Both make the next detection better.

    Threat Hunters — look forward

    Experts who proactively search for hidden threats that have bypassed existing controls. Their work is hypothesis-driven: they find threats before they become full incidents.

    They do: build hypotheses from intel and trends · hunt fileless malware, zero-days, insiders · map behaviour to ATT&CK and close detection gaps · write and tune rules · build playbooks · validate hypotheses against IOCs. Tools: SIEM, EDR, TIPs (MISP, OpenCTI), Zeek, Wireshark, ATT&CK Navigator, Python/PowerShell.

    Digital Forensics — look back

    Investigate incidents, analyse digital artefacts and find the root cause. They specialise in data recovery, malware analysis and evidence preservation for legal or internal use.

    They do: collect evidence and keep the chain of custody · image systems · disk, memory and network forensics · recover deleted files · static and dynamic malware analysis · report for audits and courts. Tools: Volatility, FTK Imager, Autopsy, EnCase, Ghidra, Wireshark, CloudTrail.

    Hunting vs incident response

    Threat huntingIncident response
    Starts fromA hypothesis — no alertAn alert or a report — something happened
    Question"Is an attacker already here, unseen?""What happened, and how do we stop it?"
    TimingBefore the damage — proactiveAfter detection — reactive
    OutputNew detections, closed gaps — or a new incidentContained, eradicated, recovered systems
    Dwell timeterm
    Definition
    The time an attacker stays inside the network before anyone detects them.
    Why it exists
    The longer the dwell time, the more the attacker can explore, steal and prepare. Reducing it is a main goal of hunting.
    Where used
    Hunting reports, incident reports, SOC metrics and yearly industry reports.
    Example
    An attacker got in on the 1st; a hunt finds them on the 15th → 14 days of dwell time.
    SOC example
    A hunter's hypothesis — "the backup account is used at night" — finds an intruder no alert caught. The hunt becomes a new SIEM rule: tomorrow it is a Tier 1 alert.
    Keep this

    Hunter = before the alert. Forensics = after the breach. The hunter turns findings into new rules; forensics turns evidence into facts that hold up.

    Working with the red team and the threat-intel team

    The red team shows the SOC what it misses. The threat-intel team tells the SOC what is coming. Both feed straight into better detection.

    Red team

    Simulates real-world attacks to test the defences. It emulates the TTPs of real threat actors to find weaknesses.

    Threat-intel team

    Gathers, analyses and distributes threat intelligence so the organisation can understand, detect and respond to new threats — it enriches data, informs detection and supports hunting.

    Purple teaming

    Red and SOC (blue) teams work together, in real time: the red team runs an attack while the SOC observes, detects and tunes. It finds detection gaps and improves alert accuracy faster than a covert test.

    A threat-intel warning, end to end

    1. 1 · TI teamIdentifies a new ransomware variant targeting the financial sector.
    2. 2 · TI → SOCShares the IOCs and attack vectors with the SOC.
    3. 3 · SOCIntegrates the IOCs into SIEM detection rules.
    4. 4 · AnalystsMonitor for suspicious file hashes and unusual PowerShell activity.

    Triage, then investigation

    Triage answers "is it real and how urgent?" in minutes. Investigation answers "how, how far and why?" — it takes longer and goes deeper.

    Event triage

    Analysing and categorising alerts to decide if they are a potential incident, a false positive or benign — quickly judge relevance and severity, and pick the next step.

    Event investigation

    Analysing validated events to find the root cause, scope and impact — enough to understand the attack, stop it, and prevent it again.

    Triage — 5 activities
    1 · Alert reviewread the SIEM / EDR / IDS alert
    2 · Filter false positivesdismiss routine events
    3 · Severity assessmentimpact and risk level
    4 · Initial investigationlogs, user activity, system behaviour
    5 · Escalation decisionescalate or close
    Investigation — 5 activities
    1 · Root cause analysisphishing? malware? how it started
    2 · Attack scopesystems, users, data affected
    3 · Threat huntingrelated activity, attacker footprints
    4 · Forensics — collectionlogs, memory dumps, captures

    Escalation and communication

    Escalation moves an incident to the right people at the right time. Communication makes sure everyone knows only what they should, when they should.

    Escalationterm
    Definition
    Handing an incident to the right team or authority when its complexity, severity or impact is bigger than the first responder can handle.
    Why it exists
    Speed up response, limit damage, keep handling consistent, meet compliance, and keep trust with stakeholders.
    Where used
    Every SOC shift — Tier 1 → Tier 2/3 → IR/CSIRT → executives, legal, PR, regulators.
    Example
    Ransomware on a server holding customer data goes straight to the IR team, CSIRT and management.
    SOC example
    Tier 1 sees a confirmed compromise of the critical file server. It is beyond Tier 1's authority — the analyst documents it and escalates to Tier 2 within minutes.
    Escalate when…

    it exceeds your capability or authority · it touches critical infrastructure or sensitive data · it spreads fast or hits many systems · external stakeholders or regulators must be involved · it could cause reputational, financial or legal damage.

    Tier 1 in practice — escalate when…

    1 it is a major attack that needs IR or forensics · 2 a fix is needed that you cannot do (remove malware, isolate a host, reset a password) · 3 someone outside the SOC must be told (management, customers, partners) · 4 you do not understand it.     Threat intelligence — feeds and platforms

    Intelligence tells the SOC who attacks, how they attack, and which artefacts they leave. Feeds bring it in; platforms organise it.

    Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI)term
    Definition
    Collecting, analysing and sharing information about potential or current threats to the organisation — actors, their TTPs, and IOCs.
    Why it exists
    So the SOC can defend proactively: know what to look for before it hits.
    Where used
    SIEM enrichment, alert prioritisation, threat hunting, vulnerability patching.
    Example
    A feed lists a new ransomware's file hashes and C2 domains.
    SOC example
    The hashes go into a SIEM rule. Two days later the rule fires on a finance laptop — before encryption starts.

    Not all intelligence is equal. Blocking a hash is easy for you — and easy for the attacker to change. Detecting a TTP forces the attacker to change how they work.

    Incident response inside the SOC — and handling vs response

    Incident response is a core SOC function. Incident handling is the bigger frame around it — the two words are often mixed up.

    IR role in the SOCWhat it means
    Detection & monitoringWatch networks, endpoints and systems with SIEM, EDR, IDS/IPS; decide if alerts are real incidents
    TriageSeverity, scope and impact; classify Low / Moderate / High / Critical and prioritise
    InvestigationRoot cause, scope, impact — logs, endpoint forensics, network analysis, threat intel
    Containment · Eradication · RecoveryLimit the spread · remove malware and persistence, patch · restore from clean backups and validate
    Post-incident review · DocumentationLessons learned · timelines, actions, mitigations; reports for leadership, legal, regulators
    External coordinationLaw enforcement, regulators, consultants; breach-notification requirements
    How IR works with the rest

    SOC analysts → escalated alerts · TI → IOCs and TTPs for context · Red team ← lessons from real incidents · SOAR → automates isolation and blocks · Management & compliance ← detailed reports.

    Common IR problems

    Alert fatigue — too many false positives · skill gaps in forensics and malware · limited visibility from missing logs · lack of automation slows response.

    Incident handling vs incident response

    What INE says

    Incident Handling is the broader term: managing an incident from start to finish — preparation, planning and coordination. Incident Response is a subset of incident handling: the actions to mitigate and resolve the incident after detection.

    MITRE adds

    The two terms are used inconsistently across SOC communities; many SOCs call the whole function "Incident Response" and include tracking and reporting in it.

    MITRE, 11 Strategies of a World-Class Cybersecurity Operations Center (2022).

    AspectIncident HandlingIncident Response
    ScopeBroad: planning, detection, response, recovery, improvementNarrow: detection, response, containment, recovery
    EmphasisPreparation, coordination, overall managementTactical response and remediation
    GoalA strong foundation for handling incidentsRespond fast and limit impact
    ActivitiesPolicies, procedures, training, incident managementInvestigation, containment, eradication, recovery, forensics
    DurationOngoingEvent-driven — starts when an incident occurs
    WhoIncident Management Team (SOC managers, IR coordinators, compliance/policy)IRT or SOC analysts (Tier 1–3), forensic analysts, threat hunters
    Memory hook

    Incident response asks the technical question: "What happened — and how do we stop it?" Incident handling asks the management question: "How do we run the whole thing — before, during and after?"

    Six pitfalls seen in real incidents

    Weak hardening (easy entry) · missing logs (no visibility) · poor alerting (logs, but no rule) · wrong scope (one host cleaned, three missed) · no clear owner (everyone waits) · untested backups (recovery fails). Each one maps to a pillar you met in Part 1.

    Keep this

    Handling is the whole programme, always on. Response is the action, triggered by an incident.

    SOAR and playbooks

    SOAR runs the response for you. Playbooks tell it — and the analyst — exactly what to do, in what order, and who decides.

    Orchestration

    Coordinates actions across tools (SIEM, EDR, firewalls) so they work as one.

    Automation

    Runs repetitive tasks — triage, enrichment, response — with less human work.

    Response

    Playbooks and workflows for a fast, consistent, standard response.

    Case management

    One place for all incident data — tracking, documenting, reporting.

    How SOAR works — five steps

    • 1Alert ingestion from SIEM, IDS/IPS, EDR and TI platforms.
    • 2Automated triage & enrichment — threat intel, geolocation, reputation.
    • 3Automated response from playbooks — isolate endpoints, block IPs/domains, notify stakeholders.
    • 4Case management & documentation — every action logged, analysts collaborate.
    • 5Post-incident review — analytics and reports to improve next time.
    FeatureSOARSIEM
    Primary roleAutomates and orchestrates responseAggregates and analyses event data
    FocusResponse and automationDetection and correlation
    AutomationResponse actions via playbooksLimited — mainly alerting
    PlaybooksCustom response workflowsNone
    Use caseFaster, more efficient responseDetecting threats through log analysis
    Exam link

    SOAR and SIEM are complementary: SIEM detects and analyses; SOAR automates and orchestrates the response.

    Playbooks

    What INE says

    A playbook is a predefined, structured set of workflows and actions that guides analysts through responding to specific incidents — what to do, in what order, and by whom.

    Why use them

    Standardisation · speed and efficiency · less alert fatigue · simpler decisions under pressure · teaches junior analysts · supports compliance.

    How they run

    Detect → trigger the matching playbook (SOAR or analyst) → automated actions → manual decision points → final response (contain, eradicate, recover) → documentation and reporting.

    ▶ Two example playbooks

    Click to open each one.

    1. Ingest the suspicious-email alert (SIEM or mailbox monitoring).
    2. Analyse automatically — sender domain reputation, headers and attachment hashes vs threat intel, links in the body.
    3. Contain — if malicious, quarantine it from all mailboxes and block the sender domain.
    4. Notify the recipient why the email was malicious.
    5. Update detection with the new indicators.
    6. Document — generate the incident report automatically.
    1. Ingest the malware alert from EDR or SIEM.
    2. Enrich — check the hash in threat intel; find the origin (phishing, drive-by download).
    3. Contain — isolate the endpoint, block known C2 servers.
    4. Remediate — automated scan, remove or quarantine the files.
    5. Recover & validate — confirm the endpoint is clean, reconnect it.
    6. Document — record every action and write the report.

    Objective: analyse and respond to potential phishing automatically.

    Objective: detect and contain malware on endpoints automatically.

    Keep this

    SIEM detects → SOAR responds → the playbook says how. Automate the repeatable; keep people on the decisions.
