    S05 · P04Incident story⏱ 6 min

    Two Companies, One Ransomware

    Same attack, same day. One lost two weeks. One lost two hours.

    Company A saw the alert at 02:00. Nobody knew who to call. The IT engineer rebooted the server "to clean it" — destroying the memory evidence. No one wrote anything down. Management heard about it two days later from a customer. Recovery took two weeks, and they could never prove what was stolen.

    Company B saw the same alert. The analyst opened the playbook for ransomware, created a case, isolated the machine from the network but left it powered on, captured memory, and notified the IR manager per the escalation matrix. Recovery from clean backups took two hours. The report showed exactly what happened.

    The difference was not skill

    Both had good analysts. Company B had done the preparation — a plan, a playbook, an escalation list, tested backups, and a rule about not powering off. That is what this session teaches.

    S05 · P05Theory⏱ 10 min

    Event · Alert · Incident — Say It Correctly

    These three words are not the same, and using them wrongly confuses everyone.

    WordSimple meaningExample
    EventAnything that happened. Usually harmless.A user logged in.
    AlertAn event a rule found suspicious.A user logged in 50 times and failed.
    IncidentA confirmed event that harms or threatens the business.The attacker succeeded and is inside.
    Easy way to remember

    Event = something happened. Alert = a machine thinks it's suspicious. Incident = a human confirmed it's bad. Only a human declares an incident.

    What counts as an incident?

    Anything that breaks one of the three security promises — C.I.A.:

    PromiseBroken when…Example incident
    Confidentialitysomeone sees data they shouldn'tCustomer database stolen
    Integritydata is changed without permissionWebsite defaced, records edited
    Availabilitya service stops workingRansomware, DDoS
    SOC Perspective

    Not every alert becomes an incident — most are false positives. But every incident started as an alert someone took seriously. That is why triage quality decides everything downstream.

    S05 · P06Theory ⭐⏱ 14 min

    Three Frameworks — NIST vs SANS vs EC-Council

    Students always ask "which one is correct?" — all of them. They describe the same journey.

    NIST — the four phases

    SANS — the six steps

    Notice the loop at the bottom. SANS draws the process as a cycle: what you learn in step 6 goes straight back into step 1, so the next incident is handled better. NIST means the same thing — SANS just makes it visible.

    EC-Council — the nine steps

    How they line up

    NIST (4)SANS (6)EC-Council (9)
    PreparationPreparationPreparation
    Detection & AnalysisIdentificationRecording & Assignment · Triage · Notification
    Containment, Eradication & RecoveryContainment · Eradication · RecoveryContainment · Evidence Gathering · Eradication · Recovery
    Post-Incident ActivityLessons LearnedPost-Incident Activities
    Which do I use?

    NIST is our reference (and the exam's). SANS is the same thing with the middle split — useful because real teams talk about "containment" and "eradication" separately. EC-Council adds the paperwork steps (ticket, notify, evidence) that actually happen in a real SOC.

    Common student mistake

    Thinking more steps = a better framework. No — a small team may use NIST's 4 phases perfectly. The framework is just a checklist so you don't forget a step under pressure.

    S05 · P07Phase 1⏱ 14 min

    Phase 1 — Preparation

    The only phase you do when nothing is wrong. It decides how the other three go.

    The document trio — the part students confuse most

    DocumentAnswersLengthFire analogy
    PolicyWhat and why — the rule1–2 pages"This building must be fire-safe."
    PlanWho and in what order10–30 pagesThe fire escape plan on the wall
    PlaybookExactly how, for one incident type2–5 pages each"If the kitchen burns: do these 6 things"
    Say it in one line

    Policy = the rule. Plan = the strategy. Playbook = the steps. Management signs the policy, the IR lead writes the plan, the senior analyst writes the playbooks.

    Also prepared before anything happens

    • Severity matrix — what counts as Low / Medium / High / Critical (agreed, not argued at 3 AM).
    • Escalation matrix — which severity wakes which person.
    • Contact list — IT, management, legal, HR, and the external IR firm if you have one.
    • IR toolkit / jump bag — imaging tools, write blocker, clean USBs, notebook.
    • Tested backups — a backup you never restored is not a backup.

    S05 · P08Phase 1⏱ 12 min

    The Team — CSIRT, CERT, and Who Does What

    Lots of names for similar teams. What matters is that every phase has an owner.

    NameStands forUsually means
    CSIRTComputer Security Incident Response TeamThe company's own IR team
    CERTComputer Emergency Response TeamOften national/government level
    SIRT / IRT(Security) Incident Response TeamSame idea, different company naming
    SOCSecurity Operations CenterWatches 24/7, escalates to IR
    Where incident response actually happens — L2, not L3

    Students often assume "L3 = the incident responder." That is wrong. Incident response and digital forensics sit with L2 — they are the ones who investigate the confirmed incident, contain it and carry out the forensics. L3 is threat hunting and detection engineering, a different job.

    RoleMain jobOwns which phases
    SOC L1Monitor the queue, triage alerts, escalateDetection
    SOC L2 · IR & DFInvestigate, respond, contain, do the forensicsAnalysis → Containment → Eradicate/Recover → Report
    SOC L3Threat hunting, detection engineering, mentoringAnalysis (supports the rest)
    Management / legalApprovals, disclosure, legal dutiesReport

    Remember it this way: L1 finds it, L2 fixes it, L3 hunts for what nobody reported yet.

    In a small company

    Often the same three people are the SOC, the IR team and the IT team. That is fine — but the roles must still be written down, because during an incident you need to know who decides to shut down a production server.

    RACI in one example

    "Isolate an infected laptop" → R: SOC L2 (does it) · A: IR manager (answers for it) · C: IT team (asked first) · I: the user's manager (told after).

    S05 · P09Phase 1⏱ 12 min

    Playbooks — The Steps, Written Down

    So a new analyst at 3 AM does the same good job as your best analyst.

    Why a plan is not enough

    The IR plan says "containment actions will be taken." That sentence is useless at 3 AM. The playbook says: "block the sender domain on the mail gateway, block the file hash on the EDR, remove the email from all inboxes." That is the difference — granularity.

    What every playbook contains

    PartMeaning
    Prerequisiteslogs, fields, rules and tools that must exist first
    Trigger (use cases)which alerts start this playbook
    Detection & analysis stepswhat to check, in order
    Containment / eradication / recoveryexact response actions
    Roleswhich step is L1, which is L2, which needs approval
    Workflow diagramthe picture — usually the most-used page
    One playbook per incident type

    Phishing · Malware · Ransomware · Account compromise · Data exfiltration · Insider · Policy violation. A hundred detection rules may all lead to the same phishing playbook — that is normal.

    S05 · P10Example⏱ 12 min

    A Real Phishing Playbook

    Follow the diagrams — this is what a working playbook actually looks like.

    Step 1 — Detection: what starts it

    Step 2 — Analysis: what the analyst checks

    • Who sent it, who received it? Is the receiver a high-value target (finance, HR, CEO)?
    • Pull out the URLs, attachments, QR codes.
    • Check sender domain age and reputation — and look for typosquatting (Session 4 skills!).
    • Check hashes and links on VirusTotal / URLScan.
    • Hunt for anyone who clicked — suspicious logins after the email arrived.

    Step 3 — Containment: stop the damage

    StageActions for phishing
    Containblock sender address, domain and IP on the mail gateway · block file hashes on EDR · block the URL on the proxy
    Eradicatedelete the email from every inbox · if someone clicked: isolate the machine, kill sessions, reset the password, start the malware playbook
    Recoverreset credentials + enable MFA · review what the account did · reimage affected machines
    Notice the chaining

    The phishing playbook can call the malware playbook. Real playbooks connect to each other — that is normal and good.

    S05 · P11Phase 2⏱ 14 min

    Phase 2 — Detection & Analysis

    Three small flows: write it down → decide how bad → tell the right people.

    How to read every flow in this session — learn this once

    All eight process flows use the same symbols. Learn them here and every diagram becomes easy to follow.

    SymbolMeans
    RectangleAn action — do this
    DiamondA question — the flow splits here
    Green ✓ circleThe YES branch
    Red ✗ circleThe NO branch
    Gold circle “…”Connector — the flow continues from / into another phase
    Thick gold outlineThe most important step on that diagram
    Blue boxA team or an outside input

    Tip for teaching: put the diagram on screen, then trace one path aloud from the top connector to the bottom connector before explaining any of the boxes.

    Flow 1 · Recording & Assignment — "no ticket, no incident"

    What this means, simply: an incident can arrive two ways — a person reports it, or a machine detects it. Either way it must become a written case with a number, a time and one named owner. If two people own it, nobody owns it.

    The two questions in the diamonds are there for good reasons:

    QuestionWhy we ask itIf the answer is unexpected
    Does it need a response?Filters out noise and duplicatesNO → still record it, then close. The count matters for reporting.
    Was it closed before?Spots a returning attackerYES → reopen the old case instead of starting fresh, so the history stays together.
    Example

    Last month you closed "suspicious login from Romania — user was travelling." Today the same alert fires for the same account. Because you reopen rather than open a new case, you instantly see it is the second time — and suddenly it looks much less like travel.

    Flow 2 · Triage — is it real, is it ours, how urgent?

    What this means, simply: first prove the evidence is real (Session 4's true/false positive call). Then say what type it is — malware, phishing, insider — because the type decides which playbook you open. Then check it is even your team's job. Finally, set the severity.

    "Outside our scope" is a real branch

    Someone stealing office laptops is a security problem, but not a cyber one — that goes to Facilities. An employee watching videos all day goes to HR, not the SOC. Knowing what is not yours keeps the queue clean.

    Example

    Alert: "user downloaded 4 GB to a USB drive." Validate — it really happened. Classify — possible data theft (insider). Outside our scope? Partly: the SOC investigates the technical facts, but HR and Legal own the person. Severity: High, because data may already be gone.

    Flow 3 · Notification — the right people, in the right order

    What this means, simply: you do not get to tell the whole company (or the press) yourself. Management is told first and approves who else hears about it. The escalation matrix — written back in Preparation — already says who that is, so nobody argues at 3 AM.

    Two traps in this flow

    1 · Do not use compromised email. If the attacker is in the mail system, they will read your incident emails. Switch to phone or a separate chat.
    2 · Record who you told and when. After a real breach this becomes a legal question, and "I think I called someone" is not an answer.

    S05 · P14Phase 3⏱ 16 min

    Phase 3 — Contain, Collect, Eradicate, Recover

    The most dangerous phase — because this is where people destroy their own evidence.

    Containment — stop the bleeding

    TypeMeaningExamples
    Short-termStop it spreading right nowIsolate the host, block the IP, disable the account, kill the session
    Long-termA safe temporary state so business continuesPatch, segment the network, tighten firewall rules

    Flow 4 · Containment — three kinds of response

    What this means, simply: containment is not only a technical job. The flow asks three separate questions, and an incident can answer YES to all three at once — each task goes to a different team, and they work in parallel.

    BranchWho actsWhat they actually do
    TechnicalSOC / ITisolate the host, block the IP, disable the account, kill sessions
    ManagementManagersapprove taking a production server offline, decide to tell customers
    LegalLegal / compliancebreach-reporting duties, evidence rules, contracts, regulators
    Example — ransomware on a finance server

    Technical: isolate the server from the network. Management: approve the downtime — finance cannot work today. Legal: customer data may be involved, so the regulator may have to be told within 72 hours. All three branches fire on the same incident.

    The loop at the bottom is the honest part

    "Contained? → NO → escalate → go back to the strategy." Real containment often fails on the first try — the attacker has a second foothold you did not know about. The dashed line means try again with more people, not give up.

    The mistake that ruins investigations

    Do not power off or reboot to "clean it fast." That deletes RAM — running processes, injected malware, network connections, sometimes the encryption key. Instead: disconnect from the network but leave it ON until memory is captured.

    Flow 5 · Evidence gathering — and how far to take it

    What this means, simply: the left column is the technical work you do. The diamonds at the bottom are business decisions made by management, not by you — but they are only possible because your report is solid.

    DecisionMeaning in plain wordsWhere it leads
    Do we know who did it?Did the forensics identify a person or group?NO → consider hiring specialists
    Hire outside investigators?Is this beyond our skill or capacity?YES → a DFIR firm takes over, then reports back
    Law enforcement required?Is this a crime we must report?YES → police · NO → internal HR discipline
    Example — two very different endings

    An outsider ransomwares your company → law enforcement, and the evidence must survive court. An employee copies the client list before resigning → same forensic work, but it ends with HR and the company lawyer, not the police. Same evidence, different door.

    Why chain of custody appears so early

    Notice it is step 2 — immediately after collecting, before any analysis. If you start analysing first and document later, you can never prove the evidence was untouched in between.

    Eradication — remove them completely

    Delete the malware, close the backdoors, remove the attacker's accounts, reset stolen credentials, reimage if needed. Then the important part: fix the root cause. If they got in through an unpatched server and you only delete the malware, they will be back tomorrow.

    What this means, simply: two things make this flow different from "just delete the virus."

    • The escalate loop — if you cannot remove the cause (a vendor bug, no patch yet), you do not pretend it is fixed. You escalate and come back.
    • The "other systems" check — the single most-skipped step in real incidents.
    Example

    An old web server was breached through an unpatched service. You clean it and feel finished — but the company runs nine more servers with that same version. If you skip the "similar systems" branch, tomorrow's incident is already waiting.

    Flow 7 & 8 · Recovery, then Post-Incident

    What recovery means, simply: one question decides the path — was data lost? If yes, restore from backup first, then restart services. If no, just restart. Either way you keep watching afterwards, because attackers commonly try to come back through a door you missed.

    The backup trap

    A backup taken after the attacker got in already contains their malware or backdoor. Always check that your restore point is older than the intrusion — which means you need the timeline from the evidence phase. This is why the phases are in this order.

    What post-incident means, simply: five steps — document what happened, measure the damage, update the policies that failed, disclose if you legally must, and formally close the case.

    Disclosure is not the analyst's decision

    Telling customers, regulators or the public is decided by legal and management — often with a legal deadline (for example 72 hours under GDPR). But they decide based on your report. Vague report → bad decision.

    S05 · P15Phase 4⏱ 12 min

    Phase 4 — Post-Incident Activity

    The phase everyone skips — and the only one that makes you stronger.

    The meeting rule

    Blameless

    The question is never "who clicked the link?" — it is "why was it possible for a click to cause this?" If people fear blame, they hide incidents, and hidden incidents become disasters.

    What must come out of it

    OutputWhy
    Incident reporttimeline, impact, actions taken — for management and legal
    New detection ruleso the same attack alerts earlier next time (back to Session 4!)
    Updated playbookwhatever was missing or wrong during the response
    Training / awarenessfor staff or for the analysts
    The circle closes here

    Post-incident feeds straight back into Preparation. That is why the lifecycle is drawn as a loop, not a line — every incident should leave the SOC better than it found it.

    S05 · P19SOC Lab⏱ 30 min

    Lab — Run a Case From Start to Finish

    Scenario: suspected data exfiltration over FTP. Build the case an auditor would accept.

    Environment

    In-class TheHive VM (TryHackMe · TheHive Project). Log in with the analyst profile given in the room. You also get a PCAP file to look at in Wireshark.

    1. Look at the evidence. Open the PCAP in Wireshark. Find the FTP traffic and note the source IP doing the transfer.
    2. Create the case. Title it clearly ("Suspected data exfiltration over FTP"). Set Severity, TLP and PAP, and add tags (data exfiltration, ftp).
    3. Add tasks that match the lifecycle — for example:

      "Analyse PCAP" · "Identify data transferred" · "Contain the host" · "Notify manager" · "Write report"

    4. Add the MITRE TTP. Tactic = Exfiltration, technique = T1048.003 (exfiltration over unencrypted non-C2 protocol).
    5. Add the observable. Type = IP, value = the source IP you found. Tick Is IOC, add a short description.
    6. Close the loop. Complete a task, write a comment explaining your verdict.

    ✅ Verification

    • Your case appears in the case list with the correct severity and TLP
    • At least 3 tasks exist, and one is marked done
    • The case has a MITRE technique attached
    • At least one observable is flagged as an IOC
    Think while you click

    Ask yourself at each step: which NIST phase am I in right now? Creating the case = Detection & Analysis. Adding "contain the host" = Phase 3. The tool is just the lifecycle with buttons.

    S05 · P20Automation⏱ 12 min

    SOAR — Let the Robot Do the Boring Part

    Security Orchestration, Automation and Response.

    The three letters, in plain words

    LetterMeansExample
    O — Orchestrationconnect all your tools togetherSIEM + threat intel + firewall + ticket system in one screen
    A — Automationthe playbook runs by itselfno analyst clicks needed for the repetitive checks
    R — Responsetake action from that one screenblock IP, disable user, isolate host
    Playbook vs SOAR playbook

    An IR playbook is a document a human follows. A SOAR playbook is that same document turned into automated steps. You cannot automate what you never wrote down — which is why Preparation comes first.

    SOAR does not replace analysts

    It handles repetitive work. It cannot make judgement calls, understand business context, or respond to a new kind of attack. And humans are the ones who write the playbooks it follows.

    S05 · P22Forensics⏱ 12 min

    Forensic Readiness — Why It Belongs Here

    Forensics is not a separate job. It is a way of working during the incident.

    Digital forensics = finding and analysing evidence on digital devices in a way that survives scrutiny — from your manager, an auditor, or a court.

    Simple example

    Police raid a suspect's home and take a laptop, a phone and a USB stick. The forensics team finds a map of a bank on the laptop, escape routes in a document, and chat records on the phone. Same skills — we just do it for company incidents instead.

    The four phases (NIST)

    PhasePlain meaning
    1 Collectiongather the devices/data safely, without changing anything
    2 Examinationfilter the mountain of data down to what matters
    3 Analysisconnect the pieces into a timeline of what happened
    4 Reportingwrite it so both an engineer and a manager understand

    Types of digital forensics

    Computer · Mobile · Network · Database · Cloud · Email — Sessions 6–9 take these one at a time, each with its own incident type.

    S05 · P23Forensics⏱ 14 min

    Handling Evidence So It Still Counts

    Collect in the right order, prove nobody changed it, never write to the original.

    Order of volatility — grab what is dying first

    Some evidence disappears in seconds; some is still there next month. Always collect the fastest-disappearing thing first.

    OrderEvidenceLost when…
    1CPU registers & cacheinstantly
    2RAM / memory ⭐the machine powers off
    3Network connections, ARP cacheseconds to minutes
    4Running processes, open filesthe process ends
    5Disk — files, registry, logs ⭐survives reboot
    6Backups, archived logsslowest of all
    This is why we said "don't power off"

    Items 2, 3 and 4 all live in memory. One reboot deletes them permanently — including the running malware you were trying to catch.

    Two forensic images

    ImageContainsVolatile?
    Memory imageRAM — running processes, connections, injected codeYes — take it first
    Disk imageeverything on the drive, bit by bitNo — survives restart

    Chain of custody — the evidence logbook

    A form that records: what the evidence is, who collected it, when, where it is stored, and every person who touched it since. One gap and the evidence can be thrown out.

    Write blockers

    Plug a suspect's drive straight into your PC and the operating system will quietly touch it — changing timestamps and ruining your evidence. A write blocker sits in between and physically allows read only.

    Hashing = the proof

    Hash the evidence when you collect it. Hash it again later. Same hash = nothing changed. That single number is what makes your evidence trustworthy.

    S05 · P25Knowledge check⏱ 10 min

    Knowledge Check — Response, Tools & Evidence

    Q1 A host is infected. What should you not do first?

    • Disconnect it from the network
    • Capture a memory image
    • Power it off and reboot to clean it
    • Open a case and assign an owner

    Q2 Which do you collect first — memory or disk?

    • Disk — it holds more data
    • Memory — it disappears on shutdown
    • Either, order does not matter
    • Neither — collect backups first

    Q3 What is the chain of custody for?

    • Choosing which tool to use
    • Blocking the attacker's IP
    • Deciding incident severity
    • Proving evidence was never altered

    Q4 Why use a write blocker?

    • It lets you read the drive without changing it
    • It speeds up imaging
    • It encrypts the evidence
    • It deletes malware automatically

    Q5 In TheHive, what is the difference between TLP and PAP?

    • TLP is severity, PAP is priority
    • TLP = who you can share with, PAP = what actions you may take
    • They mean the same thing
    • TLP is for malware, PAP is for phishing

    Q6 Does SOAR replace SOC analysts?

    • Yes, fully
    • Yes, for all L1 work
    • No — it automates repetitive work only
    • No — SOAR cannot take any action

    Q7 You deleted the malware but the attacker returns next day. What was skipped?

    • Fixing the root cause during eradication
    • Setting the case severity
    • The chain of custody form
    • The TLP marking

    S05 · P27Takeaways⏱ 3 min

    Key Takeaways

    • An incident is confirmed harm — a machine raises alerts, a human declares incidents.
    • NIST, SANS and EC-Council describe the same journey; we use NIST's 4 phases.
    • Preparation decides everything. The plan you didn't write is the one you'll need at 3 AM.
    • Policy = rule · Plan = strategy · Playbook = the exact steps.
    • Record → triage → notify. No ticket, no incident.
    • Contain before you clean; collect before you contain. Never power off — isolate.
    • Eradication without root cause just invites them back tomorrow.
    • Post-incident is blameless and feeds straight back into preparation.
    • Evidence needs volatility order, chain of custody, a write blocker and a hash — or it's worthless.
